Change Management Skill Builder

Free course · 6 modules · About 95 minutes

For nonprofit leaders. Turn the way you already lead change into a Claude skill you and your team can reuse.

What's a Claude skill?

A Claude skill is a saved set of instructions that teaches Claude how to do one specific task your way. You add it to your Claude account once. After that, whenever you ask for that kind of task in any chat, Claude recognizes it, loads your instructions, and follows them, so you don't have to explain your process again.

A prompt
The message you type in one chat. Claude uses it for that conversation only. To get the same approach next time, you have to explain it again.
A skill
Instructions saved to your account. Claude uses them whenever it recognizes the task, in any chat. You can share the file so colleagues get the same approach.

In this course, you'll build a skill of your own

You'll base it on how you already lead change and on the people your organization serves, works with, and answers to. The next time something changes, Claude will help you plan who to tell, in what order, and what to say, the way you would.

For reviewers · 3 minutes

How this course was designed

A short case study: who it's for, what it's built to do, the choices behind it, and how it was tested. Designed by James D. Rodgers, built with Claude as a design and build partner.

The problem

Nonprofit leaders, especially at organizations of 20 to 60 staff, lead change often, usually without a communications team. Most already know how to tell people well: who hears first, who needs a phone call, what not to put in an email. That knowledge lives in their heads, and it gets skipped when things move fast.

Generic AI advice doesn't help much, because it doesn't know their people or their rules. This course teaches them to turn their own method into a Claude skill, so Claude follows it every time.

Scope

The skill plans change communication: who to tell, in what order, and what to say. That is one part of change management, not all of it. I chose it because it's where small organizations most often get hurt: staff learning about their own jobs from an all-staff email, or a funder hearing news secondhand.

The approach

PrincipleWhere it shows up
Backward designI started from the end product, a working skill in the learner's own Claude account, and the behaviors that show it works. Those became the objectives and the Module 5 grading checklist.
Merrill's First PrinciplesActivation: Module 2 starts from a real change the learner led. Demonstration: Module 1 shows two answers side by side. Application: Modules 3 to 5. Integration: Module 6, where they hand the skill to a colleague.
Contrasting casesModule 1 puts a one-off prompt next to a skill on the same situation, and asks learners what they notice before showing them.
Critical incident techniqueModule 2's walk-back questions pull the learner's method out of one real event instead of asking them to describe it in the abstract.
Adult learningEverything uses the learner's own organization and their own account. They leave with something they'll use the next week.
Feedback tied to actionIn Module 5, each No on the grading checklist names the part of the skill to fix and what to ask Claude.

Key decisions

  • Learners work in their own Claude account. They keep the skill, and nothing about their organization is sent to this site. The trade-off is a few setup steps, which Modules 3 and 4 walk through.
  • No AI runs on this site. The Module 1 examples are written ahead of time. That keeps the course free to run and private. The trade-off is no personalized feedback in Module 1, so learners compare their notes with an expert list instead.
  • One prompt builds the whole skill. An earlier version had learners fill in each part of the skill with checkboxes. It worked, but it was long, and testing suggested people would drop off. Now Claude writes the skill from their Module 2 answers, shows it in full, and only packages it once the learner has read it.

How it was tested

I tested the course in walkthroughs with simulated learners: leaders of a youth orchestra, a food bank, a health clinic, an animal shelter, an arts center, and others. The cases covered thin answers, a phone screen, answers written in Spanish, unusual formatting in Claude's replies, and learners who edit their work after drafting. Testing led to these changes:

  • The learner's own rules now go into the skill word for word, instead of through Claude's rewording.
  • The course checks the skill file before upload, and catches problems like a missing folder or a description that would break the file.
  • The page now gives clear instructions at each step, and a plain message when something goes wrong.
  • Module 3 went from many checkboxes to one prompt.

Accessibility: keyboard use throughout, real headings for screen readers, labeled controls, color contrast checked, and layouts tested at phone width. Next step: a pilot with real nonprofit leaders.

How success is measured

  • Reaction: the optional feedback form at the end.
  • Learning: a working skill that passes the Module 5 grading checklist.
  • Behavior: the two-week check-in with a colleague in Module 6: is the skill being used on real changes?

Why bother?

The situation

Imagine you're in the following situation:

You lead a 35-person youth services nonprofit. Your largest foundation grant, which paid for most of your after-school tutoring program, wasn't renewed. You plan to close the program by spring. Your board votes on the closure next week.

  • 4 program staff
  • 22 volunteer tutors
  • 60 students and their families
  • 2 partner schools
  • 3 smaller funders who also support the program

You ask Claude for help planning how to tell people.

Two answers to the same request

Your turn: what did you notice about these two responses?

Compare the two answers. What did the one-off answer miss, and what would that mean for a 35-person organization? A few things to look at:

  • Who hears about the closure, and in what order?
  • Does anyone get a private conversation before the news goes wide?
  • Did it add anything you didn't tell it?
  • What did it get right?
See our full list
  1. Everyone hears at once. In a 35-person organization, the order people hear matters. Staff who learn about their own jobs from the same email as families can feel it as a breach of trust.
  2. The program staff hear with everyone else. Their jobs are ending. They need a private, in-person conversation before anyone else knows.
  3. Funders and schools are treated like the public. A funder who learns about a closure from a group email, or a school that hears at the same time as its students' families, has reason to question the relationship.
  4. No review flag. Ending jobs and ending a service to families can carry legal and HR obligations. A plan should stop and say so.
  5. It filled a gap with an invented fact. "We explored options" may be true, but you didn't say it. If it isn't true, it's now in a message with your name on it.
  6. What it got right. It noticed the board hadn't voted and suggested waiting. That's a good start, but it stopped there instead of planning around the vote.

When is it worth it to use a skill?

Why a skill

  • Build it once, use it on every change
  • It follows your judgment, not generic advice
  • You can hand it to a colleague

When it isn't worth it

A one-time task doesn't need a skill. A skill earns its keep when you do something again and again and want it done your way.

If you only want Claude to keep background about your organization in mind, a Claude Project with instructions may be enough. A skill is better when you want a set method followed in any chat, and when you want to hand that method to colleagues.

Step 4Your organization's rules

A rule is something you do every time there's a change, the way your organization does it. Write as many as you need. Some questions to get you started:

  • Looking at your groups, who always hears first? Who hears last?
  • Is there anyone who should never hear by mass email or newsletter?
  • What has to happen before anything goes public?
  • Who has to approve a message before it goes out?
  • What did you learn from your change in Part 1 that you'd want to do every time?

Step 5What's unique about your organization

For example: a union contract, families who speak languages other than English at home, a government contract with notice requirements, a congregation you share space with, or a small community where news travels fast.

Why not Kotter or ADKAR?

Established change models are well researched and worth reading. We don't build on them here for two reasons. They were designed for large organizations with change teams and long timelines. And the point of this course is to put your judgment into the tool. If a formal model helps you, borrow from it as you write your rules.

Step 4Read it before you export it

Do this: Read the skill file Claude showed you. Look for these four things. If one is off, tell Claude what's wrong in the same chat and ask for a corrected version.

  • The description names the task and the kinds of change you'd use it for
  • Your rules are there, word for word

Upload your skill

Step 1What Claude needs

Claude only accepts a skill packed a certain way. If you used the prompt in Module 3, yours already is. Knowing the rules helps when an upload fails.

The right shape 
change-planning-v1.0.zip
└─ change-planning/
   └─ SKILL.md
A common mistake 
change-planning-v1.0.zip
└─ SKILL.md

The file is loose in the .zip, with no folder around it. Claude rejects this.

  • One folder inside the .zip, with the same name as your skill: change-planning
  • SKILL.md inside that folder. This is the file Claude reads.
  • A header at the top of SKILL.md, between two lines of three dashes, with two lines:
    • name: lowercase letters, numbers, and hyphens only, up to 64 characters
    • description: up to 200 characters. Claude reads it to decide when to use your skill.
Check my file

Choose your .zip. This page reads it in your browser and checks the rules above. Nothing is uploaded.

    Step 2Make sure skills are turned on

    Skills work on every Claude plan, including Free. They need code execution turned on.

    Free, Pro, Max, or Ultra

    Go to Settings > Capabilities and turn on Code execution and file creation. In newer layouts it's called Create and edit files. You did this in Module 3.

    Team or Enterprise

    An owner goes to Organization settings > Plugins & skills, opens the Policy tab, and turns on both code execution and Skills. If you aren't an owner, ask yours.

    Step 3Upload and turn it on

    1. In Claude, go to Customize > Skills
    2. Click +, then + Create skill
    3. Choose Upload a skill
    4. Choose your .zip file
    5. Find your skill in the list and turn on the switch next to it. A skill that's switched off isn't available to Claude.

    Step 4Confirm it works

    Do this: Start a new chat and name the skill, so you know it loads. Then read Claude's reply: it should use your six output sections.

    Use my change-planning skill. We're changing our office hours next month. Help me plan how to tell people.

    In Module 5, you'll test whether Claude uses it on its own, without being told.

    If something goes wrong

    What you seeWhat to do
    No Skills section in CustomizeCode execution is off. Turn it on (Step 2).
    Skills are greyed outCode execution is off for your account or your organization. On Team or Enterprise, ask an owner.
    The upload failsRun "Check my file" in Step 1. The usual causes are a missing folder, a folder name that doesn't match the skill name, a missing SKILL.md, or symbols the name doesn't allow.
    You can't create or upload skills at workYour organization may have turned off skills people make themselves. Ask an owner.
    Claude doesn't use itCheck that the switch is on. Then name the skill in your request. If Claude still skips it, the description needs work; Module 5 covers that.

    Updating it later

    When you fix your skill in Module 5, you'll upload the new version the same way. Then turn off the old one, or delete it: open it in Customize > Skills, switch it off, click ..., and choose Delete. Only keep one version switched on.

    From Anthropic's help articles: Use skills in Claude and How to create custom skills. Menus change; check there if a step doesn't match.
